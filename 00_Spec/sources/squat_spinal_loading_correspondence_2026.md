# Squat Spinal Loading: 2026 Letter and Author Response

## Provenance

Two separate publications, read on 2026-10-08; this node records the exchange and does not treat it as an experiment or systematic review.

- Jan-Paul vanWingerden. Letter to the Editor. IJSPT 2026;21(5):456–458. DOI [10.26603/001c.161268](https://doi.org/10.26603/001c.161268), PMID 42083619.
- Rachel K. Straub and Christopher M. Powers. Author Response. IJSPT 2026;21(5):459–461. DOI [10.26603/001c.161270](https://doi.org/10.26603/001c.161270), PMID 42083629.

## Disagreement and clarification

The letter challenges the original commentary’s account of lumbar flexion, compression tolerance and shear control, and disputes its low-back-pain recommendations. Its argument emphasizes differences between posture, trunk inclination, carried load and passive-tissue participation. The letter’s recommendation against squat use during low-back recovery is the correspondent’s position, not an established universal rule.

The authors distinguish loaded human squatting from cadaveric, passive-flexion and simplified lifting models. They clarify that lumbar flexion alone was not intended as the primary determinant of spinal forces and that squat prescription for low-back pain was meant to be individualized, not universal. They continue to defend their neutral-spine guidance within that context.

## Canonical treatment — Level 5 engine policy

Preserve both positions and their task contexts. This exchange does not validate either a camera-based spinal-risk score or an automatic prescription. Do not convert the original Figure 2 caption into “lumbar flexion is dangerous,” or the letter into “squats are contraindicated for all low-back pain.” Review the underlying studies separately before adjudicating mechanisms or clinical effectiveness.
